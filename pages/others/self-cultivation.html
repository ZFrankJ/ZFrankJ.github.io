向内求：一场迟来的自我修行

—— 再度回首开悟之旅有感

← Back to Notes

Journal entry — Sunday, August 2, 2026

过去的我试图通过大量阅读接近全知全能。我不断向外求知，仿佛只要读得足够多，就能够穷尽世界。

转折发生在2024年的一天。那天的课程排满了十二个小时，我却仍然挑战自己读完了三本十万字以上的严肃书籍。可就在第二天，我第一次如此清楚地意识到：大脑并不是一个可以无限填充的容器。知识的摄入存在代价，理解、记忆与调用都需要有限的心智资源。仅仅不断向外索取，最终只会撞上自身的边界。

也正是在那时，我发现，除了认识世界，还有另一个同样重要的方向：认识那个正在认识世界的人。

“这不是很奇怪吗？我们只能看见自己的外在，然而几乎所有事情都发生在内在。”

— 《男孩、鼹鼠、狐狸和马》

我们只能直接看见自己的外在，然而几乎所有真正重要的变化，都发生在内部。于是，我开始向内求。

所谓开悟，并不是突然获得了所有问题的答案，而是观察者的位置发生了变化。过去，我只是借助大脑观察世界；此后，大脑本身也成为了被观察、被理解和被预测的对象。当我能够看见自己的情绪从何而来、偏好如何形成、注意力为何转移，又有哪些未经审视的习惯正在支配行动时，我便第一次真正看见了自己的心智模型。

开悟之后，我所看见的不是作为器官的大脑，而是那个由经历、记忆、欲望、判断和身份共同构成的“我”。

我逐渐意识到，在不违背法律、道德以及人的基本天性的前提下，许多主观体验并非不可改变。人们常用“我喜欢”作为理由，但喜欢本身并不是终点，喜欢为何形成才更值得追问。一种爱好、一种习惯乃至一种自我认同，往往来自长期经历的反复强化。购物多了，可能逐渐依赖购物带来的满足；阅读多了，也可能在理解世界的过程中获得稳定的愉悦。

这些经历最后会凝结成定义：“我是一个怎样的人”“我喜欢什么”“我必须做什么”。定义帮助我们迅速行动，却也在无形中占据心智资源。一个人拥有的定义越多，需要维护的自我叙事便越庞杂；有些定义早已失去价值，却仍然以习惯、情绪和执念的形式延续，继续支配一个人的选择。

因此，改变习惯未必只是依靠意志与旧有欲望正面对抗。另一种方法，是回到习惯背后的理由，解除它与自我之间的绑定。不是反复告诉自己“我不能这样做”，而是不再把它定义为快乐、需要或者“我的一部分”。当旧的理由逐渐失效，习惯对心智资源的占用便会降低；而当留下的空间被更有价值的经历填补，新的生活方式也会随之生长。

在2024年大约半年的向内探索中，我不断观察自己，拆解自己，也重新塑造自己。我开始允许许多无关紧要的好恶保持未定义，不再要求每一种体验都成为身份的一部分；也开始主动放下已经失去价值的知识和执念，让它们自然淡出意识。这个过程并不总是轻松，因为向内观察意味着既是被审视的人，也是审视者；既要承认过去的自己如何形成，也要决定此后愿意成为什么样的人。

而在此之前，为了追求全知全能，我已经积累了大量跨领域知识。这些广泛的探索让我看见不同道路，也让我最终找到了最值得投入的方向。此后，我对自己的求知过程进行了一次“退火”：从试图知道一切，逐渐收束到量化研究与人工智能，将有限的心智资源集中于少数真正重要的长期项目。

向内求之后，我反而发现，学习本身并不是最困难的事情。只要拥有足够的数据、反馈与练习，人就能通过投入心智资源逐渐拟合一项任务。真正困难的是学习发生之前的决定：我究竟应该为它分配多少资源？

什么是有价值的？什么是必要的？什么虽然有趣，却不值得此刻投入？即使一个人已经看清自己，不再轻易被外界评价支配，这些问题也不会消失。相反，它们将伴随身份、环境与人生阶段的变化，被一次次重新提出。

这也让我逐渐明白，成长并不只是年龄的增加，更是责任主体的转变。学生时代的世界相对简单：学习、生活、与人交往，已经构成了日常的大部分。成年之后，却要面对工作、家庭、水电煤、车贷房贷、信用卡、保险、洗衣做饭…… 无数琐事都会要求一部分注意力，也会把原本完整的时间切割成细小的碎片。

而我的开悟来得稍晚。等我真正看清自己想做什么时，高中生活已经所剩不多。那是我在成年以前最后一段纯粹的自由时光，也是最后一段尚未被众多现实责任分割、仍然可以完整交给理想的时间。因此，我更希望趁它尚未结束，全力去做自己真正想做且认为有价值的事情。不是为了逃避未来，而是因为我知道，人生中如此纯粹的阶段不会再有第二次。

唯有这样，才不负韶华。

但刚刚开悟时，我也曾走向另一个极端。我认为只有量化与人工智能最重要，其他学业似乎都可以让路。直到2025年，我看到自己的数学成绩只有八十九分。为了把成绩提高到至少九十分以上，我不得不重新分配脑力资源，认真对待这项当时并不位于兴趣中心、却属于学生身份的任务。

这一分成为了一个转折。

它让我明白，价值并不只由喜好决定，也由责任决定。每个人在特定的人生阶段，都承担着一些无法随意取消的必要任务。学生需要完成学业，成年人也必须掌握生活与职业所要求的能力。属于身份责任主体的部分，必须获得基本而充分的资源；在此基础之上，剩余的心智空间才能投入喜好、理想以及那些可能产生长期价值的事业。

我曾以为，看清自己之后，只需抛弃所有无关之物，沿着唯一重要的方向前进。后来才发现，真正的修行并不是从世界中抽身，而是在身处世界、承担责任的同时，仍然不忘自己为何出发。

因此，向内求不是一个已经完成的阶段。那半年的探索只是起点。此后人生的每一个阶段，我都要重新审视自己的欲望、责任与方向，判断应当保留什么、放下什么，又该把有限的心智交给什么。

开悟没有替我完成此后的选择。它只是让我第一次看见了选择发生的过程，并由此拥有重新塑造自己的可能。

向外求，让我看见世界；向内求，让我看见自己。而修行，便是在看清来路之后，仍然一次次选择成为那个由我主动塑造的自己。

Journal entry — Sunday, August 2, 2026

I once tried to approach omniscience through extensive reading. I kept seeking knowledge outside myself, as though reading enough would allow me to exhaust everything the world contained.

The turning point came one day in 2024. I had twelve hours of classes scheduled, yet I still challenged myself to finish three substantial, serious books, each exceeding a rough English-language equivalent of 60,000–70,000 words. The next day, however, I realized with unprecedented clarity that the brain is not a vessel that can be filled without limit. Taking in knowledge has a cost: comprehension, memory, and recall all draw on finite cognitive resources. Simply continuing to reach outward will eventually bring us up against our own limits.

It was then that I discovered another direction, one just as important as understanding the world: understanding the person who is doing the understanding.

“Isn't it odd. We can only see our outsides, but nearly everything happens on the inside.”

— The Boy, the Mole, the Fox and the Horse

We can directly see only our exterior, yet nearly every change that truly matters occurs within. And so I began to seek inward.

Awakening did not mean suddenly possessing the answers to every question. It meant that the observer's position had changed. Before, I had simply used my brain to observe the world; afterward, the brain itself became something to observe, understand, and predict. When I could see where my emotions came from, how my preferences formed, why my attention shifted, and which unexamined habits were governing my actions, I saw my own mental model clearly for the first time.

What I saw after awakening was not the brain as an organ, but the “self” formed collectively by experience, memory, desire, judgment, and identity.

I gradually realized that, within the boundaries of law, ethics, and basic human nature, many subjective experiences are not immutable. People often say “I like it” as a reason, but liking is not the end of the inquiry; why that liking formed is the more important question. An interest, a habit, or even a sense of identity often arises through repeated reinforcement over a long period. Shop often enough, and one may grow dependent on the satisfaction shopping provides; read often enough, and one may find a stable pleasure in understanding the world.

Eventually, these experiences crystallize into definitions: “This is who I am,” “This is what I like,” and “This is what I must do.” Definitions help us act quickly, but they also occupy cognitive resources without our noticing. The more definitions a person holds, the more elaborate the self-narrative that must be maintained. Some definitions have long since lost their value, yet persist as habits, emotions, and attachments that continue to govern one's choices.

Changing a habit, then, need not rely solely on pitting willpower directly against an old desire. Another approach is to return to the reason behind the habit and loosen the bond between it and the self. Instead of repeatedly telling myself, “I must not do this,” I can stop defining it as pleasure, necessity, or “part of who I am.” As the old reason loses its force, the habit occupies fewer cognitive resources. When the space it leaves is filled by more valuable experiences, a new way of life can begin to grow.

During roughly six months of inward exploration in 2024, I continually observed, dismantled, and reshaped myself. I began allowing many inconsequential likes and dislikes to remain undefined, rather than demanding that every experience become part of my identity. I also began deliberately releasing knowledge and attachments that no longer had value, allowing them to fade naturally from awareness. This process was not always easy, because looking inward means being both the person under examination and the examiner: acknowledging how the former self came to be while deciding who the future self is willing to become.

Before this, in my pursuit of omniscience, I had accumulated a great deal of knowledge across many fields. Those wide-ranging explorations showed me different paths and ultimately helped me find the directions most worthy of commitment. I then put my pursuit of knowledge through a kind of “annealing”: gradually narrowing the attempt to know everything into quantitative research and artificial intelligence, and concentrating my finite cognitive resources on a small number of truly important long-term projects.

After turning inward, I found that learning itself was not the hardest part. Given enough data, feedback, and practice, a person can gradually fit themselves to a task by investing cognitive resources. The truly difficult decision comes before learning begins: how many resources should I allocate to it?

What is valuable? What is necessary? What may be interesting, yet not worthy of investment right now? Even after a person sees themselves clearly and is no longer easily governed by external judgment, these questions do not disappear. Instead, they return again and again as identity, circumstances, and stages of life change.

This also led me to understand that growing up is not merely an increase in age, but a shift in who bears responsibility. The world of a student is relatively simple: study, daily life, and relationships already make up most of each day. Adulthood, however, brings work, family, utilities, car and home loans, credit cards, insurance, laundry, cooking, and countless other practical demands. Each claims a portion of our attention and cuts what was once uninterrupted time into smaller fragments.

My awakening came somewhat late. By the time I truly understood what I wanted to do, little of high school remained. It was my last period of pure freedom before adulthood, and the final stretch of time not yet divided by numerous practical responsibilities—time I could still devote wholly to an ideal. While it remained, I wanted to give everything to what I genuinely wanted to do and believed was worthwhile. This was not an attempt to escape the future, but a recognition that such a pure stage of life would never come again.

Only in this way could I live up to my youth.

Yet immediately after awakening, I also moved toward another extreme. I believed that only quantitative research and artificial intelligence truly mattered, and that every other subject could make way for them. Then, in 2025, I saw that my mathematics score was only eighty-nine. To raise it to at least ninety, I had to reallocate my cognitive resources and take seriously a task that was not then at the center of my interests, but was nonetheless part of my responsibility as a student.

That single point became a turning point.

It taught me that value is determined not only by preference, but also by responsibility. At every stage of life, each person carries necessary tasks that cannot simply be cancelled. Students must complete their education; adults must master the abilities required by life and work. The responsibilities attached to one's role must receive a basic yet sufficient allocation of resources. Only then can the remaining cognitive space be devoted to interests, ideals, and work that may create lasting value.

I once believed that after seeing myself clearly, I needed only to discard everything irrelevant and proceed along the one path that mattered. I later discovered that genuine self-cultivation does not mean withdrawing from the world. It means living within the world, bearing responsibility, and still remembering why one began.

Seeking inward, therefore, is not a completed phase. Those six months of exploration were only the beginning. At every later stage of life, I must reconsider my desires, responsibilities, and direction: what to preserve, what to release, and where to commit my finite mind.

Awakening did not make the choices that followed on my behalf. It simply allowed me, for the first time, to see the process by which choices are made—and, through that sight, to gain the possibility of reshaping myself.

Seeking outward allowed me to see the world; seeking inward allowed me to see myself. Self-cultivation is the practice of seeing clearly where I came from and still choosing, again and again, to become the self I consciously shape.
