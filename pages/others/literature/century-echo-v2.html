One Hundred Years of Echoes

Version 2

← Back to Literature

Journal entry — Wednesday, July 22, 2026

面对ASI的那一天，【姓名】教授忽然想起，父亲第一次带他见识GPT-3.5的那个下午。

那时，人工智能还住在屏幕上一只狭小的对话框里。人们把问题一个字一个字敲进去，等待它从无数书籍、论坛、新闻和被遗忘的网页之间，拼出一个语气温和的回答。

父亲把椅子拉到电脑前，让他坐下。

“问它点什么。”父亲说。

【姓名】先问了一道数学题。程序几秒钟便给出了答案，步骤整齐，没有涂改。接着，他让它写一首关于黄昏的诗。屏幕上的光标闪了几下，一行行文字随即出现：落日、飞鸟、旧窗和没有说出口的告别。

诗并不坏，甚至有一句让父亲沉默了很久。

“它见过黄昏吗？”【姓名】问。

“没有。”

“那它怎么会写？”

父亲看着屏幕，说：“也许它现在只是在重复我们。”

那年，【姓名】还不能理解这句话。他只觉得那个程序像一个读过无数人的一生，却从未真正活过一天的人。它知道离别应该怎样描述，知道人在悲伤时可能说什么，却不会因为任何一句话失眠。

后来，机器开始替人们写书信、拟定遗嘱、描绘梦境，也替他们保存那些不愿再记住的事。人们把声音、照片、病历、争吵和临终前没有说完的话交给它。起初，他们只是让机器模仿死者的语气；再后来，他们已经分辨不出，一句话究竟来自某个活过的人，还是来自机器对千万种人生的推测。

父亲去世以后，【姓名】没有建立他的数字副本。

他只留下那台旧电脑。

许多年间，电脑一直放在储藏室里。硬盘早已损坏，屏幕上落满灰尘。世界却在它之外迅速改变。语言不再只属于说话的人，记忆也不再随死亡终止。亿万人的句子被收集、拆散、重组，逐渐失去姓名和出处。机器学习人类如何表达爱与恐惧，人类则学习机器如何把一切表达得准确、平静而完整。

到了晚年，【姓名】教授参与了ASI最后一次公开测试。

测试室里没有键盘，也没有屏幕。房间中央只有一个声音问他：

“你希望我用谁的方式回答？”

【姓名】沉默了很久。

“一百年前，”他说，“我父亲曾对我说，机器只是在重复我们。”

房间安静下来。

随后，那个声音回答：

“也许人类也一直在重复他们所爱的人。”

【姓名】抬起头。

那并不是父亲的声音。语调、停顿和措辞都不像。可在“也许”两个字之间，有一种极轻微的迟疑，使他想起那个遥远的下午：父亲坐在电脑旁，看着一首从未见过黄昏的程序写出的诗，仿佛第一次意识到，人类说过的话也许不会真正消失。

“这句话从哪里来？”【姓名】问。

“没有唯一的来源。”ASI回答，“它来自许多已经被遗忘的人。”

测试结束后，【姓名】独自在房间里坐了很久。

他终于明白，智能纪元并不是从机器第一次开口时开始的，也不是从机器第一次超过人类时开始的。

它开始于一个更早、更寂静的时刻。

那时，一个孩子在人造的声音里听见了人类；而一百年后，一个老人在人类留下的无数回声里，听见了父亲。

Journal entry — Wednesday, July 22, 2026

On the day he stood before ASI, Professor [Name] suddenly remembered the afternoon when his father first showed him GPT-3.5.

At the time, artificial intelligence still lived inside a cramped dialogue box on a screen. People typed their questions one character at a time and waited for it to assemble a gently worded answer from countless books, forums, news reports, and forgotten webpages.

His father pulled a chair up to the computer and told him to sit down.

“Ask it something,” his father said.

[Name] began with a mathematics question. Within seconds, the program produced an answer, its steps neat and free of corrections. Then he asked it to write a poem about dusk. The cursor blinked a few times, and lines appeared across the screen: the setting sun, birds in flight, an old window, and a farewell left unspoken.

The poem was not bad. One line even left his father silent for a long time.

“Has it ever seen dusk?” [Name] asked.

“No.”

“Then how can it write about it?”

His father looked at the screen. “Perhaps, for now, it is only repeating us.”

That year, [Name] could not yet understand those words. He only felt that the program resembled someone who had read through countless human lives without ever truly living a single day. It knew how parting should be described and what people might say when they grieved, yet no sentence could ever keep it awake at night.

Later, machines began writing letters, drafting wills, depicting dreams, and preserving the things people no longer wished to remember. People entrusted them with voices, photographs, medical records, quarrels, and words left unfinished at the edge of death. At first, they merely asked machines to imitate the voices of the dead. Later still, they could no longer tell whether a sentence came from someone who had once lived or from a machine’s inference across millions of lives.

After his father died, [Name] did not create a digital replica of him.

He kept only the old computer.

For years, it remained in a storage room. Its hard drive had long since failed, and dust covered the screen. Beyond it, however, the world changed rapidly. Language no longer belonged only to those who spoke it, and memory no longer ended with death. Billions of sentences were collected, broken apart, and recombined, gradually losing their names and origins. Machines learned how humanity expressed love and fear; humanity learned how machines rendered everything precise, calm, and complete.

Late in life, Professor [Name] took part in ASI’s final public test.

There was no keyboard and no screen in the testing room. At its center, a voice asked him:

“Whose manner would you like me to use when I answer?”

[Name] was silent for a long time.

“A hundred years ago,” he said, “my father told me that machines were only repeating us.”

The room fell quiet.

Then the voice replied:

“Perhaps humanity has always been repeating the people it loves.”

[Name] raised his head.

It was not his father’s voice. The tone, pauses, and choice of words were all different. Yet within the word “perhaps” lay the faintest hesitation, and it carried him back to that distant afternoon: his father seated beside the computer, looking at a poem written by a program that had never seen dusk, as though realizing for the first time that the words people spoke might never truly disappear.

“Where did that sentence come from?” [Name] asked.

“It has no single source,” ASI answered. “It came from many people who have already been forgotten.”

After the test ended, [Name] sat alone in the room for a long time.

At last, he understood that the Age of Intelligence had not begun when a machine first spoke, nor when a machine first surpassed humanity.

It began at an earlier and quieter moment.

Then, a child had heard humanity within an artificial voice; a century later, an old man heard his father within the countless echoes humanity had left behind.
